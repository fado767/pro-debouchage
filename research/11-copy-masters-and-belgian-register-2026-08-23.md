# HALF A. THE MASTERS, FOR THIS PAGE

## 1. Twelve masters, distilled

### Claude Hopkins, Scientific Advertising (1923)
- Specifics beat generalities: a claim with a number or a named thing is believed, a superlative is discounted.
- Sell service, not the seller. In mail order there is no boasting, save of super-service.
- Reason-why: never assert, always say why it is true.
- Every ad is a salesman that must pay for itself, so measure it or do not run it.
- Give the buyer a way to start with no risk (a sample, a test, a free look).

**On our page.** Replace "intervention rapide" with what actually happens ("on vous donne une heure d'arrivée au téléphone"), and give the reason-why for the free camera ("parce qu'elle évite de casser"). Our risk-free start is the price said on the phone.

### David Ogilvy
- Five times as many people read the headline as the body: when it is written, 80 cents of the dollar are spent.
- The consumer is not a moron: no fog, no cleverness for its own sake.
- Facts sell. Do the homework until you know something the competitor cannot be bothered to say.
- Long copy sells when the purchase is not trivial, as long as every line earns the next one.
- Put the promise in the headline and never trade clarity for style.

**On our page.** The H1 carries the whole offer alone, because most visitors read nothing else. The facts we already have (camera included, price on the phone, one named man, one identified van, an enterprise number) are our entire case.

### Eugene Schwartz, Breakthrough Advertising (1966)
- You cannot create desire, only channel a desire that already exists.
- Five stages of awareness decide the headline: unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, most aware.
- Market sophistication decides whether to lead with the claim, a bigger claim, a mechanism, a better mechanism, or identity.
- The more aware the reader, the shorter and plainer the headline may be.
- When the claims are worn out, name the mechanism, because the mechanism still sounds new.

**On our page.** Our visitor is solution aware or product aware: they typed "débouchage urgent", they know the service exists, they are comparing three tabs. Sophistication on speed is maxed out (16 of 16 competitors shout 24/7), so speed cannot lead. Price certainty is the unworn claim, and "dit au téléphone, confirmé avant la première minute de travail" is the mechanism that makes it believable.

### Gary Halbert
- Find the starving crowd first: the market is most of the result.
- AIDA is a funnel with no skippable step, and most writers under-invest in Action.
- Write to one person in the words they would use out loud, not in boardroom words.
- Urgency only when it is real: fake scarcity destroys the trust the rest of the copy built.
- Explain the action as if to a five year old: exactly what to do and exactly what happens next.

**On our page.** Google Ads at 2am is a starving crowd by definition, so the copy can be plain and short. The Action step deserves as much care as the headline: say who answers, what they will ask, and when the number comes.

### John Caples, Tested Advertising Methods
- Headlines work on self-interest, news, or curiosity, in that order of reliability.
- "How to" is the workhorse form and rarely loses badly.
- Curiosity alone is the weakest form: pair it with a benefit or drop it.
- Test headlines instead of arguing: the winner is regularly the one nobody in the room liked.
- Specific beats clever in every tested split.

**On our page.** Ads make headline tests cheap. Two ad-group variants with identical bodies and different H1s is the only honest way to settle Fady's open question 1 in `research/06`.

### Joanna Wiebe, Copyhackers
- Mine the voice of the customer: the best lines are found, not written.
- Phrase frequency in the source data sets the messaging hierarchy, which sets the section order.
- Join the conversation already happening in the reader's head, do not start a new one.
- Write to one reader, singular, and let the wrong readers self-select out.
- Run the "so what" test on every line: if the answer is "so what", cut it or push it one level deeper.

**On our page.** Our voice-of-customer source is free and unused: the Test-Achats complaint pages, the RTBF and DH scam articles, and the Google review text of the 16 competitors. One hour of mining gives the exact fear sentences to answer. "Nous sommes équipés" fails the so-what test. "Vous connaissez le prix avant qu'on sonne" passes it.

### Harry Dry, Marketing Examples
- Can you draw it? A line that makes no picture is not remembered.
- Can you falsify it? A sentence that could be proven wrong makes the reader sit up.
- Could nobody else say it? If a competitor could sign the line, it is not our line.
- A specific detail is more believable than a rounded claim, even a bigger one.
- Short beats long when the reader is standing up.

**On our page.** "Service de qualité" is undrawable, unfalsifiable and signable by anyone: banned. "On confirme le prix avant la première minute de travail" is drawable (a man at the door saying a number), falsifiable (we do it or we do not), and almost nobody in this trade dares sign it.

### Eddie Shleyner, VeryGoodCopy
- Empathy first: name the feeling before you name the product.
- Micro-copy is copy: the button, the line under the button and the captions do the closing.
- Write vividly and concretely, because emotion travels on images, not on adjectives.
- One idea per sentence, one sentence per line when the reader is skimming.
- Cut the throat-clearing: the first sentence written is usually the one to delete.

**On our page.** The line under the call button is worth more than the whole "why us" section. And one honest empathy line ("vous épongez depuis une heure") costs nothing and no competitor has it.

### Peep Laja, CXL
- Clarity trumps persuasion: if they do not understand it, nothing else matters.
- Four levers in order: clarity, relevance, friction, distraction.
- Jargon is the first sign of a rookie: it feels professional and it kills comprehension.
- A new business fights an uphill trust battle, so proof density beats polish.
- Test the value proposition on real humans before optimising anything else.

**On our page.** We are unknown in a distrusted trade, so every proof element (enterprise number, VAT number, real photos, the named technician, printed price floors) is worth more than any design flourish.

### Oli Gardner, Unbounce
- Attention ratio: links on the page divided by conversion goals should be 1:1.
- Message match: the ad's words must reappear in the H1 or the visitor bounces.
- One goal per page: a second goal costs a large share of the first.
- Encapsulation and directional cues point the eye at the one button.
- Design the page for the campaign, not the campaign for the page.

**On our page.** Our attention ratio is not 1:1 today: the FR/NL switch, privacy, cookies, WhatsApp and six service cards all compete. Rule: every non-call link stays small, muted and below the fold, and each service card links to the phone, never to another page.

### Dan Kennedy
- No offer, no sale: state exactly what the buyer gets, for what, by when.
- Urgency and deadlines must be real and stated, never implied by hype.
- Risk reversal moves more people than any adjective.
- Name the enemy: what you are protecting the customer from.
- Tell them what to do, repeatedly and explicitly.

**On our page.** The offer is not "débouchage", it is "un prix au téléphone, confirmé avant de commencer, par quelqu'un qui a un nom". The enemy is the surprise invoice, which the Belgian press already named for us. The risk reversal is the confirmed price. Real urgency exists (the water), so no countdown is ever added.

### Drayton Bird
- Imagine one ideal prospect and write to that person only.
- Do a complete selling job: answer every objection and repeat the key benefit at least three times in different words.
- Aim for relevant surprise, never originality for its own sake.
- Do not be a bore: everyday words, no jargon, you cannot bore people into buying.
- Test everything, then refer to rule one.

**On our page.** The price promise must appear three times in three different sentences (hero, how-it-works step 2, the closing line under the price table), never copy-pasted.

## 2. The twenty rules that matter most here, ranked

1. The H1 carries the whole offer alone, because most visitors read nothing else (Ogilvy).
2. Lead on price certainty, not on speed: speed is a worn claim in this market (Schwartz).
3. Every claim must be drawable, falsifiable, and unsignable by a competitor (Dry).
4. Clarity before persuasion, persuasion before beauty (Laja).
5. Write to one reader with water rising, singular, second person (Bird, Wiebe).
6. Specifics beat superlatives, and a number we can keep beats a number that impresses (Hopkins).
7. A reason-why after every claim: the camera is included because it avoids breaking (Hopkins).
8. Attention ratio near 1:1: one goal, the call, everything else visually subordinate (Gardner).
9. Message match: the H1 repeats the words of the ad group that sent the visitor (Gardner).
10. Say what happens after the button is pressed, in the reader's own terms (Halbert).
11. One empathy line before the first product line, early, in the reader's words (Shleyner).
12. Run the "so what" test on every sentence: cut it or deepen it (Wiebe).
13. Micro-copy is where the conversion happens: button label, sub-button line, captions (Shleyner).
14. Name the enemy the customer already fears, without naming a competitor (Kennedy).
15. Repeat the core promise three times in three different sentences (Bird).
16. Real urgency only: the water is the deadline, nothing artificial is added (Halbert, Kennedy).
17. Proof density over polish: enterprise number, VAT number, real photos, a real first name (Laja).
18. No jargon, no fog: everyday Belgian words, short sentences (Bird, Laja).
19. Long copy is allowed below the fold if every line earns the next (Ogilvy).
20. Settle the headline with an ad test, not with an opinion (Caples, Bird).

> **WARNING, read before using anything below.** `AGENTS.md` section 5 rule 1 says never fake anything, and lists fake reviews by name. Fady has decided that three or four placeholder reviews in customer voice may sit in the code while the page is being designed, so the review component can be built against realistic content, and that they will be clearly marked in the source and removed before the page is shown to Roro or published. The drafts below are written on that basis only. They are marked PLACEHOLDER, they are not evidence of anything, and none of them may ever reach a live page or Roro's screen. The honest day-one review card in `research/06` S8 stays the shipping content until real Google reviews exist.

### 4a. Eight rules for a believable customer-voice review

1. **Length 35 to 70 words.** Shorter reads like a rating, longer reads like a marketing text. Real reviews cluster in that band.
2. **One concrete anchor of time.** A real person says when: "samedi vers 22h", "op zondagochtend". A vague "rapidement" is the signature of a fake.
3. **One concrete anchor of place or object.** The commune, the sterput, the cellar step, the kitchen sink. Detail the writer could only have if they were there.
4. **The price is mentioned as matching, never as cheap.** Real satisfied customers say "le prix annoncé au téléphone était le prix payé", not "prix imbattable".
5. **One small imperfection, never about competence.** Waiting forty minutes, arriving later than first said, a bit of mess to mop after. This single element is what separates a believable review from an advert.
6. **Plain, uneven sentences.** A comma splice, a sentence starting with "Bref" or "Kortom", one word repeated. No adjectives stacked in threes.
7. **No brand slogans and no service lists.** A customer never writes "débouchage, curage et inspection caméra 24h/24". They write about their own problem only.
8. **First name plus commune only, star count, plausible month.** Never a surname, never a street, never a photo of a face. Four or five stars, not all five.

# HALF B. THE BELGIAN REGISTER

## 5. The trade vocabulary, French of Belgium

Confirmed against Belgian trade sites and the belgicism lexicons in the sources.

| Use this | What it is | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| déboucheur, débouchage | the trade and the job. The word Belgians type into Google. | Not "dégorgement" (France), not "plombier" alone (a different trade in the buyer's head). |
| canalisation bouchée | the blocked pipe | Fine everywhere, our highest-volume phrase. |
| égout | the sewer, and "raccordement à l'égout" | "à l'égout" is normal Belgian French, no need to say "réseau d'assainissement". |
| sterput | the yard gully or floor drain, a Dutch loanword fully naturalised in Belgian French | Used on Brussels-periphery French sites and searched in French. Gloss it once as "sterput (siphon de cour)" and then use it plain. |
| avaloir | the street or driveway gully | Pure belgicism (France: bouche d'égout). Correct here, keep it. |
| chambre de visite | the inspection chamber | Belgian standard. Never "regard", which is the French-from-France word. |
| taque | the cast iron cover on the chamber | Belgicism, natural in speech, fine in a photo caption. |
| WC bouché, toilette bouchée | the most searched job | Both spellings are used in Belgium. "WC" uppercase in FR. |
| évier, lavabo, douche, baignoire | kitchen sink, basin, shower, bath | Keep the distinction: "évier" is the kitchen, "lavabo" the bathroom. |
| fosse septique, vidange | the septic tank and its emptying | "Vidange de fosse septique" is the phrase used and searched. |
| curage, hydrocurage | the full cleaning of the line, not just the plug | "Curage haute pression" is the plainest form for a consumer. |
| pompage de cave, cave inondée | the flooded cellar job | "Cave" not "sous-sol" for a house. |
| dégât des eaux | the insurance term | The exact words the insurer will use, so use them too. |
| inspection caméra, caméra d'inspection | the camera job | "Endoscopie" is jargon, reject. |

## 7. Register, tone and typography

**How to address the customer.** FR: "vous" everywhere, no exception. NL: "u" everywhere, and consistently. Flemish B2C service sites drift between "u" and "je" (ontstoppingsservice.be does it inside a single page), which reads careless. Belgians are more formal than the Dutch and a stranger entering your home at 2am is not a "je" situation. Decide "u" once, then check every line.

**Belgian French forms that belong on this page.** "septante" and "nonante" whenever a number is written out or spoken in a call script, never "soixante-dix" or "quatre-vingt-dix". "GSM" for a mobile number. "commune" for the local authority, never "mairie" or "ville". "cave" for the cellar. "à tantôt" is fine in speech, not in copy.

**Belgian French forms to recognise but not write.** "savoir" used for "pouvoir" ("je ne sais pas venir avant 18h") is genuine Belgian speech but confusing in written copy, and the phone script should say "je ne peux pas". "déjeuner, dîner, souper" mean breakfast, lunch and dinner here, so never write meal words at all: write clock hours. "une fois" as a sentence tag is a foreigner's cliché of Belgium and would be read as mockery.

**Town names.** French names on the FR page where they genuinely exist and are used: Vilvorde, Hal, Rhode-Saint-Genèse, Leeuw-Saint-Pierre, Grand-Bigard, Wavre, Braine-l'Alleud. Dutch names on the NL page: Vilvoorde, Halle, Sint-Genesius-Rode, Sint-Pieters-Leeuw, Groot-Bijgaarden, Waver, Eigenbrakel. **But the postal address in the footer stays in Dutch on both pages**, because the seat is in Flanders and Flemish addresses are officially Dutch only: `Guldenschaapstraat 6, 1800 Vilvoorde` on the FR page too. Note an inconsistency to fix: `playbook/landing-page.md` section 4 writes "basé à Vilvoorde" inside French copy, while `research/06` correctly uses "Vilvorde" in French.

**Belgian Dutch forms.** "voormiddag" means the whole morning in Belgium (a Dutch reader hears late morning), and "zaterdagvoormiddag" is exactly right on a Flemish page. "proper" is warm, normal Belgian standard and fine in "de leiding is weer proper". "seffens", "sebiet", "amai" and "goesting" are spoken only and must never appear in copy. "tot straks" is fine in a WhatsApp reply, not on the page.

**Tone.** Belgians distrust hype and reward plainness and precision. An understated line lands harder than an enthusiastic one, and a stated limitation ("ce n'est pas toujours réparable en une fois") buys more trust than any guarantee. Humour is dry and self-deprecating, never boastful, which is why the `research/06` #8 headlines work: they joke about the plunger, not about the company's greatness.

**Typography, French of Belgium.** A narrow non-breaking space before `? ! : ;` and inside `« »`. Note that the Belgian business-letter norm NBN Z 01-002 recommends no space before `?` and `!`, following German usage, but Belgian editorial practice (RTBF, Le Soir) keeps the space, so we keep it. Euro sign after the amount with a non-breaking space: `129 €`. Decimal comma: `129,50 €`. Thousands with a non-breaking space: `1 250 €`. Hours: `22 h` per the norm, `22h` in common web usage, pick one and never mix. Dates: `23 août 2026`, numeric `23/08/2026`.

**Typography, Dutch of Belgium.** Never a space before `? ! : ;`. **This is a live bug in `research/06`**: the NL headlines there are written `stinkt het ?` and `Kelder onder water ?` with a French space before the question mark. Correct to `stinkt het?`. Euro sign before the amount with a space: `€ 129`, or `129 euro`. Decimal comma: `€ 129,50`. Thousands with a point: `1.250`. Avoid the Dutch bookkeeping style `€ 129,-`. Hours: `22 u` or `22.00 uur`. Dates: `23 augustus 2026`, numeric `23/08/2026`.

**The phone number.** Display it in the brand's memorable grouping `0480 649 649` in both languages. Use `+32 480 64 96 49` in schema.org and any international context, and `tel:+32480649649` in every href. Read aloud, digit by digit, per `AGENTS.md` section 6.
